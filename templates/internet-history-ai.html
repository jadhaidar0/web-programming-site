History of the Internet

Jad Haidar

The Internet is basically the cables, machines and protocols that connect computers around the world and let them send data to each other. It doesn't have one inventor or one start date. It came together slowly, with different people adding pieces over the years. Some say it started in 1969, when the first two computers were linked on ARPANET. Others say 1974, when Cerf and Kahn published the idea of connecting different networks together. I think 1983 makes the most sense, because that's when everything switched over to TCP/IP, which is still what the Internet runs on today.

Twelve verified milestones

1960s Baran 1960–64, Davies 1965–66

Packet switching is invented

What.Paul Baran at RAND and Donald Davies at NPL in the UK independently worked out how to break data into small addressed packets that travel independently instead of reserving a dedicated circuit. Davies coined the word "packet".

Why it mattered.It made a shared data network both efficient and resilient. Computer traffic is bursty, so dedicated circuits sit idle; packets let many conversations share one line, and any node failure just reroutes traffic.

A disputed attribution. Leonard Kleinrock's 1961–62 MIT work on queueing theory is often listed as a third origin, but whether it actually proposed packet switching is contested by Davies, Baran and later historians. Janet Abbate, a historian who was not a participant, credits only Baran and Davies.

Source: Abbate, Inventing the Internet, MIT Press, 1999, chapter 1.

1969 29 October, 22:30

The first ARPANET message

What.Charley Kline at UCLA attempted a login to Bill Duvall at Stanford Research Institute. The letters "l" and "o" transmitted, then the system crashed. The full login succeeded about an hour later. By December 1969 there were four nodes: UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara and Utah.

Why it mattered.This was the first operational packet-switched network and the direct ancestor of the Internet.

Sources: UCLA / Kleinrock, reproducing the original IMP log; UCLA Newsroom, Internet 50 symposium.

1971 approx.

The first network email

What.Ray Tomlinson sent the first email across a network and chose the "@" symbol to separate the user from the host.

Why it mattered.Email became ARPANET's dominant use within a couple of years, proving the network was for people communicating, not just for sharing computer time.

On the date. Tomlinson said he never recorded it, and it may have been early 1972. The date is given here as approximate.

Source: Internet Society, "A Brief History of the Internet".

1973

The first international connections

What.NORSAR in Norway and University College London joined ARPANET. Peter Kirstein led the UCL connection. In the same year, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn began designing TCP.

Why it mattered.It showed the network could cross oceans, and it exposed the real problem: connecting different networks, such as ARPANET, satellite and packet radio, to one another.

Sources: UCL, "UCL celebrates internet pioneers"; also covered in the Internet Society history above.

1974 May

The Cerf–Kahn paper

What."A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication" was published in IEEE Transactions on Communications, Vol. COM-22, No. 5. It is the paper that introduced the term "internetworking".

Why it mattered.This is the founding design document. Its key principle is to keep the network simple and put intelligence in the end hosts, so any network can join without being redesigned.

Sources: the paper (PDF, Princeton copy); DOI 10.1109/tcom.1974.1092259.

1978 to September 1981

TCP is split into TCP and IP

What.The decision to split the protocol came in 1978. The standards were published in September 1981 as RFC 791 (IP) and RFC 793 (TCP).

Why it mattered.The layered TCP/IP model is still exactly how the Internet works today.

Sources: RFC 791; RFC 793.

1983 1 January

"Flag Day": ARPANET moves from NCP to TCP/IP

What.ARPANET switched completely from NCP to TCP/IP on a single day. The changeover plan is set out in RFC 801 (Postel, November 1981).

Why it mattered.This date is widely cited as the birthday of the Internet proper. From here on, any TCP/IP network could interconnect.

Source: RFC 801, NCP/TCP Transition Plan.

1983 November

The Domain Name System

What.Paul Mockapetris at ISI specified DNS in RFC 882 and RFC 883, revised in 1987 as RFC 1034 and RFC 1035. The first .com domain, symbolics.com, was registered on 15 March 1985.

Why it mattered.It replaced a single hosts.txt file, maintained by hand at SRI and downloaded by every machine, which was already failing at a few hundred hosts. DNS made naming distributed and scalable.

Sources: RFC 882; RFC 1034.

1986

The NSFNET backbone becomes operational

What.The NSFNET backbone became operational at 56 kbit/s and was later upgraded to T1 and T3. It connected around 2,000 computers in 1986, growing to more than 2 million by 1993.

Why it mattered.It opened the Internet broadly to universities and research centres, not just defence contractors. Traffic exploded.

On the date. Some sources date the first backbone to 1985. "Backbone operational 1986" is the accurate statement.

Source: National Science Foundation, NSFNET.

1988 2 November

The Morris worm

What.A self-replicating program written by Robert Morris spread across the network, infecting an estimated 6,000 machines. CERT was created weeks later.

Why it mattered.It was the first major Internet security incident, and it made security a permanent concern of network design.

On the numbers. The commonly repeated "6,000 machines" or "10% of the Internet" was a contemporary rough estimate, not a measurement. Nobody had the instrumentation to count infections.

Source: Cornell Commission report, Communications of the ACM, June 1989 (PDF).

1995 30 April

NSFNET is decommissioned

What.The NSFNET backbone was shut down and the Internet backbone handed over entirely to commercial providers. ARPANET itself had been decommissioned in February 1990.

Why it mattered.Full privatisation. This is the point at which the Internet stopped being a government research project and became commercial infrastructure.

Sources: Internet Society, "Happy Birthday Backbone"; Merit Network (the operator), "Retiring the NSFNET Backbone Service" (PDF).

2011 3 February 2011 and 6 June 2012

IPv4 runs out; IPv6 launches

What.IANA allocated its final IPv4 blocks to the regional registries on 3 February 2011. World IPv6 Launch took place on 6 June 2012.

Why it mattered.It confirmed that the original 32-bit addressing scheme had run out and began the still-incomplete migration to IPv6.

On IPv6's dates. IPv6 was first specified in RFC 1883 (December 1995), revised as RFC 2460 (1998), and only became a full Internet Standard as RFC 8200 in 2017. It is not accurate to say it was "standardised in 1998".

Sources: Number Resource Organization, "IPv4 free pool depleted"; Internet Society, World IPv6 Launch press release.

Three key technologies

  • Packet switching

    How data travels

    Data is split into small packets, each carrying its own destination address. Packets travel independently, take different routes, and are reassembled at the far end. This replaced circuit switching, where one dedicated line was reserved for the whole conversation. It made a large shared network economically viable and resilient to failure.

  • TCP/IP

    How different networks connect

    A common language for connecting different networks. IP handles addressing and moving packets between networks through gateways; TCP sits on top and turns unreliable packet delivery into a reliable, ordered stream between two programs. This is the technology that turns a network into an internet. Because it assumes nothing about the underlying network and puts reliability in the end hosts, the same architecture scaled from a few hundred hosts to billions of devices.

  • The Domain Name System

    How names become addresses

    A distributed, hierarchical database mapping human-readable names to IP addresses, with authority delegated down the tree (root, then .com, then example.com) so that no single organisation maintains the whole thing. It replaced a hand-maintained file and made growth beyond a few hundred machines possible. URLs on the Web depend on it.
