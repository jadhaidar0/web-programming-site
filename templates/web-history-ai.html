History of the World Wide Web

Jad Haidar

A lot of people mix up the Web and the Internet, but they're different. The Internet is the network. The Web is something that runs on top of it, the same way email or online games do. The Internet had already existed for about twenty years before the Web came along. The Web was made by one person, Tim Berners-Lee, while he was working at CERN, and he wrote his first proposal for it in 1989. He had a simple problem: the scientists there kept their work on different computers that couldn't talk to each other, and things got lost whenever people moved on. CERN was already running on TCP/IP by then, so he didn't have to build a network. He only had to come up with a way to link documents together over the one they already had.

Eighteen verified milestones

1980

ENQUIRE

What.On a six-month contract at CERN, Tim Berners-Lee wrote ENQUIRE, a personal note-keeping program in which documents linked to one another. It was never released.

Why it mattered.It was the conceptual seed of the Web, linked records rather than a rigid hierarchy, nine years before the proposal.

Source: CERN FAQ, "The birth of the Web".

1984 to 1989

CERN standardises on TCP/IP

What.Berners-Lee returned to CERN as a fellow in 1984. CERN, previously running a mix of DECnet and other protocols, standardised internally on TCP/IP and by 1989 was the largest Internet site in Europe.

Why it mattered.A global open network already existed for the Web to run on. Berners-Lee did not have to invent transport, only the document layer. This is where the history of the Internet and the history of the Web join.

Source: CERN, "A short history of the Web".

1989 March

"Information Management: A Proposal"

What.Berners-Lee submitted the founding document to his manager, Mike Sendall, who annotated the cover "Vague but exciting...". It was not formally approved, but he was allowed to work on it. Nothing was built yet.

Why it mattered.It named a problem specific to CERN, with thousands of physicists rotating through, documentation scattered across incompatible machines, and knowledge lost when people left, and proposed distributed hypertext over the existing network as the answer.

Sources: the proposal itself, hosted by W3C; CERN timeline.

1990 May and 12 November

The refined proposal, and the name

What.Berners-Lee resubmitted a lightly edited version in May 1990. In November 1990 he and Robert Cailliau submitted the formal management proposal, "WorldWideWeb: Proposal for a HyperText Project", which is where the project got its name. He was given a NeXT computer, whose object-oriented toolkit is why a browser could be built in a few months.

Why it mattered.The project was named, approved and resourced.

Chronology. In May 1990 the 1989 proposal was recirculated. In September, Sendall approved the NeXT purchase. In October, Berners-Lee began coding, chose the name "WorldWideWeb", and Cailliau joined as co-author. The document cited here is dated 12 November 1990.

Sources: the joint proposal itself; CERN, "A short history of the Web".

1990 by Christmas

The three core technologies are built

What.By Christmas 1990, Berners-Lee had defined and implemented HTML (borrowing from SGML), HTTP and URLs, and had written the first browser (WorldWideWeb, which was also an editor) and the first web server, running on info.cern.ch.

Why it mattered.The Web went from proposal to working system. Everything on the Web today descends from those three specifications, which were kept deliberately simple so that anyone could implement them.

On the date. The specific 25 December date that circulates is not in any primary source. W3C and CERN both say only "by Christmas".

Sources: original HTTP/0.9 specification as implemented; earliest HTML tag list (c. 1991–92); Berners-Lee on the first browser; CERN, "The birth of the Web".

1991 6 August

The line-mode browser and the public announcement

What.Nicola Pellow wrote the line-mode browser, a text-only client that ran on any terminal. On 6 August 1991 Berners-Lee posted the software to the alt.hypertext newsgroup. In December 1991, SLAC in California ran the first web server outside Europe.

Why it mattered.The line-mode browser made the Web usable off NeXT machines, and the newsgroup post was the moment it left CERN.

Sources: the post itself (primary source, plain text); CERN, "A short history of the Web".

1992 to 1993

Spread, then Mosaic

What.Roughly 26 web servers existed at the end of 1992 and about 500 by the end of 1993. NCSA released Mosaic in 1993, not a CERN product, with inline images and Windows and Mac versions.

Why it mattered.Mosaic turned the Web from a physicists' tool into something the general public wanted to use.

Sources: W3C, "A Little History of the World Wide Web"; CERN Web@30, web history.

1993 30 April

CERN puts the Web in the public domain

What.CERN formally waived all rights to the World Wide Web software. Anyone could build servers, browsers or sites without paying or asking.

Why it mattered.This is the most consequential decision in the story. Compare Gopher: earlier in 1993 the University of Minnesota announced it might charge licence fees, and Gopher adoption collapsed almost immediately. Openness is why the Web won, and why it is a public commons rather than a product.

Sources: CERN timeline, Web put in the public domain; CERN, "Twenty years of a free and open Web"; CERN Courier on the legal side of the release.

1994 May

The first International WWW Conference, at CERN

What.Held at CERN and heavily oversubscribed, with around 400 attendees. It was nicknamed the "Woodstock of the Web".

Why it mattered.This was the point at which the Web community became larger than CERN itself.

Source: CERN Web@30, web history.

1994 1 October and 16 December

Handover to W3C

What.Berners-Lee left CERN for MIT in October 1994 and founded the World Wide Web Consortium. In December, CERN's council decided to concentrate its resources on the Large Hadron Collider and transferred Web development to W3C and INRIA.

Why it mattered.It put Web standards in a neutral, vendor-independent body exactly as the browser wars began, rather than leaving them with one physics lab or one company.

Two separate events. W3C was founded on 1 October 1994. CERN Council approved the LHC under tight budget conditions on 16 December 1994, which is when Web development passed to W3C and INRIA.

Sources: W3C, its own founding history; W3C, "A Little History of the World Wide Web".

1995 to 1996

The standards are written down

What.HTML 2.0 was published as RFC 1866 (November 1995); HTTP/1.0 as RFC 1945 (May 1996); URL syntax as RFC 1738 (December 1994). CSS Level 1 became a W3C Recommendation in December 1996.

Why it mattered.It turned informal practice into written standards that independent browser makers could implement consistently.

Sources: RFC 1866; RFC 1945; RFC 1738.

1997 to June 1999

HTTP/1.1

What.HTTP/1.1 was published as RFC 2068 in 1997 and revised as RFC 2616 in June 1999.

Why it mattered.It added persistent connections and the Host header, letting many websites share one IP address. It remained dominant for around fifteen years.

Source: RFC 2616.

2004

WHATWG forms and the Web becomes an application platform

What.Browser makers formed the Web Hypertext Application Technology Working Group, unhappy that standards work had turned toward XHTML and away from the Web as people actually built it. The same year, applications like Gmail showed that a page could update without reloading.

Why it mattered.It set the Web on the path from a document system toward an application platform, and it created the split in standards authority that took fifteen years to resolve.

Source: WHATWG.

2014 28 October

HTML5 becomes a W3C Recommendation

What.HTML5 was published as a W3C Recommendation on 28 October 2014, after roughly eight years of work. It added the semantic elements this page is built from, along with native audio, video and canvas.

Why it mattered.Before it, layout meant nested <div> elements and video meant a plug-in. HTML5 gave structure back its meaning and made media part of the language.

Source: W3C, HTML5 Recommendation, 28 October 2014.

2017

WebAssembly ships in every major browser

What.WebAssembly, a portable low-level bytecode format that runs alongside JavaScript, reached its first stable version and shipped in all major browsers.

Why it mattered.For twenty years JavaScript was the only language a browser would execute. WebAssembly made the browser a target for compiled languages too.

Source: WebAssembly.

2019 and December 2020

One HTML standard again, and the end of Flash

What.In 2019 W3C and WHATWG agreed that WHATWG's HTML Living Standard would be the single version of HTML, ending the fork that began in 2004. In December 2020, Adobe Flash reached end of life.

Why it mattered.Both settled long-running arguments in favour of the open Web: one standard rather than two, and open formats rather than a proprietary plug-in.

Source: WHATWG.

2022 June

HTTP/3 moves the Web off TCP

What.HTTP/3 was published as RFC 9114, running HTTP over QUIC instead of TCP. By 2026 it is supported by more than 95% of browsers in use and serves around 40% of the top ten million websites.

Why it mattered.Berners-Lee's original HTTP was a single line of text over a TCP connection. The meaning of a request has barely changed since; how it travels has been rebuilt three times.

Sources: RFC 9114, HTTP/3; IETF, HTTP updates.

2026

Where the Web is now

What.Around 95% of page loads in the United States are encrypted, IPv6 passed half of Google's traffic in March 2026, and HTTP/3 carries a growing share of requests. The three technologies Berners-Lee defined by Christmas 1990 — HTML, URLs and HTTP — are all still in use.

Why it mattered.Every layer beneath them has been replaced at least once, and the addressing scheme above them is being replaced now. The design held because it assumed as little as possible about what sat underneath it.

Sources: Let's Encrypt statistics; Google IPv6 statistics.

In 2013, for the twentieth anniversary of the public-domain release, CERN restored the first website at its original address: first-website.web.cern.ch and info.cern.ch.

Three key technologies

These three map onto the three things any hypertext system needs: a way to write a document, a way to name it, and a way to fetch it. Everything else, from browsers and servers to Mosaic and the W3C, is implementation or governance built on top of them.

  • HTML

    HyperText Markup Language: how you write the document

    Berners-Lee based it on SGML, which CERN already used for documentation, and kept it minimal: a handful of tags, the most important being the anchor tag that creates a link.

    Why it mattered. A plain-text format anyone could write by hand on any machine, so publishing a page required no special software.

  • URI / URL

    Uniform Resource Identifier / Locator: how you name it

    A single global naming scheme, scheme://host/path, so that any document anywhere could be pointed at unambiguously.

    Why it mattered. Berners-Lee considered this the most important of the three. One universal address format is what let the Web wrap around systems that already existed, such as FTP, Gopher and news, instead of having to replace them. Without it, links could not cross machines and the Web would just be isolated documents.

  • HTTP

    HyperText Transfer Protocol: how you fetch it

    The protocol a browser uses to ask a server for a document and receive it. The original version was tiny: a single request line, GET /path, and the server returns the file.

    Why it mattered. Simple enough that a working server was a few hundred lines of code, which is a large part of why servers spread so quickly between 1991 and 1993.

Sources for this section: Berners-Lee, "How It All Started", W3C 10th anniversary talk, 1 December 2004 (slide deck; the link opens on the cover slide); CERN, "A short history of the Web".
